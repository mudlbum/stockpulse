The income statement is a set of judgements about timing. The cash flow statement is closer to a record of what happened. When the two disagree persistently and in the same direction, the disagreement is information — and it is available for free, in tagged form, from the SEC. The [long-term board](/methodology/) scores it at a weight of 0.22 under the name CashGeneration, and this article walks through the construction, the arithmetic of a worked example, and the two situations where the ratio flatters a company it should not.

## Where the gap comes from

Net income recognises revenue when it is earned, not when it is collected, and matches costs to the period they relate to rather than the period they were paid. Those are reasonable accounting principles and they are also a large amount of discretion.

The result is that a company can honestly report growing profit while cash moves the other way. The gap has names:

**Receivables.** Revenue booked, invoice not yet paid. Growing receivables faster than revenue means the company is financing its customers — possibly because it is winning business by extending terms.

**Inventory.** Cash converted into goods that have not sold. Rising inventory ahead of rising sales is either preparation or misjudgement, and the balance sheet cannot tell you which.

**Capitalisation.** Spending recorded as an asset rather than an expense — software development, content, certain contract costs. It leaves this year's income statement intact and shows up in the cash flow statement immediately.

**Depreciation policy.** Longer assumed asset lives mean lower annual depreciation and higher reported income, with no effect on cash at all.

None of these is fraud. All of them widen the gap between reported profit and collected cash, and Sloan's 1996 finding — that the accrual component of earnings is substantially less persistent than the cash component, and that prices did not appear to reflect that — is the reason the ratio is worth a factor slot.

## The calculation

```
FCF        = operating_cash_flow_TTM − |capex_TTM|
EV         = market_cap + total_debt − cash
FCF_yield  = FCF / EV
conversion = FCF / net_income_TTM         (clipped to [0, 2])

CashGeneration = 0.6 × FCF_yield + 0.4 × (conversion / 2)
```

Several implementation notes.

**Capex is taken as an absolute value.** The XBRL tag `PaymentsToAcquirePropertyPlantAndEquipment` is reported as a positive number representing an outflow, while some filers and some fallback tags sign it negatively. Taking the magnitude avoids the case where a sign convention difference turns a capital expenditure into a capital receipt and doubles free cash flow.

**The yield denominator is enterprise value, not market capitalisation.** Free cash flow is generated by the whole business and is available to both lenders and shareholders, so it belongs over the whole capital structure. Using market cap makes leveraged companies look cheap.

**Conversion is clipped to [0, 2] and is null when net income is not positive.** A company with net income near zero and positive free cash flow would otherwise produce an unbounded ratio that dominates the factor. A loss-making company gets no conversion score at all — which is correct, since the ratio has no meaning against a negative denominator.

**The two terms are combined at raw scale, then z-scored together.** The `conversion / 2` term rescales a [0, 2] range onto [0, 1] so the two components sit on comparable scales before the composite is [sector-neutrally z-scored](/blog/sector-neutral-scoring/) across the universe. The published spec writes this as two separate z-scores blended afterwards; the implementation blends first and normalises the composite. The ordering it produces is very close, but they are not the identical operation, and it is worth knowing which one is running.

## A worked comparison

Two companies, each reporting net income of 500 for the trailing twelve months.

| | Company C | Company D |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Net income (TTM) | 500 | 500 |
| Operating cash flow (TTM) | 300 | 620 |
| Capex (TTM) | 120 | 120 |
| Free cash flow | 180 | 500 |
| **Conversion** | **0.36** | **1.00** |
| Market cap | 6,000 | 6,000 |
| Total debt | 1,000 | 1,000 |
| Cash | 400 | 400 |
| Enterprise value | 6,600 | 6,600 |
| **FCF yield** | **2.7%** | **7.6%** |

On earnings alone these companies are identical. On a price-to-earnings basis they are identical. Company C converts roughly a third of its reported profit into cash it can actually deploy; Company D converts all of it and then some, because its depreciation charge exceeds its capital spending.

Where did Company C's 200 go? The cash flow statement says. It is a specific set of line items — the change in receivables, the change in inventory, the change in payables — and each one is a question with an answer. A single year of low conversion is often ordinary: a company growing 40% has to fund working capital. Five consecutive years of it, while revenue grows 6%, is a different statement.

## What the factor sits next to

CashGeneration is one of five factors on the long-term board and it deliberately overlaps with two others.

The [Piotroski F-score](/blog/piotroski-f-score/) contains its own accruals test — operating cash flow scaled by assets exceeding net income scaled by assets. It is the same question asked as a binary rather than a magnitude, and it appears in both places because it is the single most useful check in the set.

The valuation factor uses FCF yield again, at a weight of 0.4 inside its own composite, paired with the negated log of EV/EBIT. That double-counting is intentional: cheapness on a cash basis and cheapness on an operating-earnings basis are different claims, and a company that is cheap on both is making a stronger statement than one cheap on either alone.

The [distress gate](/methodology/) is the hard version. Any company with negative operating cash flow in three of the last four quarters, or interest coverage below 1.5×, is excluded from the long-term board at any score. No amount of growth quality or cheapness overrides it.

## Two ways conversion flatters

**Stock-based compensation.** It is a real cost — the share count grows, existing holders own less — and it is a non-cash expense, so the cash flow statement adds it back to arrive at operating cash flow. A company paying a large fraction of compensation in equity therefore reports higher operating cash flow, higher free cash flow and better conversion than an otherwise identical company paying cash. The ratio does not adjust for it and neither does this pipeline. For companies where equity compensation is a meaningful share of revenue, conversion above 1.0 should be read with that in mind, and the [ultra-long model's shareholder-yield term](/blog/economic-moat-in-data/) — which computes net buyback yield from the actual share count — is where the cost eventually shows up.

**Underinvestment.** Free cash flow rises when capital spending falls. A company that cuts capex below the level needed to maintain its asset base prints excellent free cash flow for two or three years and then discovers why the spending existed. Conversion cannot distinguish a genuinely capital-light business from one that has stopped investing, because both show the same low capex. Comparing capex against depreciation over several years is the usual check, and it is not currently a factor here.

## The remaining limitations

**Trailing twelve months is four quarters.** For a company with lumpy capital spending — a manufacturer mid-way through a plant build, a shipping company taking delivery of vessels — a TTM free cash flow figure describes a phase of a cycle rather than a normal year. The [5–10 year board](/blog/economic-moat-in-data/) addresses this by scoring the *share of the last ten fiscal years with positive free cash flow* rather than the current level. The 1–2 year board does not.

**Working capital swings both ways.** A company that had a poor collections quarter shows weak conversion and may simply recover it next quarter. One year of the ratio is close to noise; the factor uses one year.

**Acquisitions are outside the measurement.** Cash spent buying companies is an investing outflow that is not capex, so it does not reduce free cash flow as defined here. A serial acquirer can show strong conversion while consuming enormous amounts of capital. The invested-capital denominator in [the ROIC factor](/blog/roic-vs-roe/) does capture it, which is one reason the two factors are scored separately rather than combined.
